BURGER: Benchmarking and Understanding Robotic Generalization via Embodied dRawing

Author list withheld — paper under double-blind review

PDF Paper arXiv Coming soon Code Coming soon Data Coming soon

Robot datasets are expensive, and most manipulation benchmarks give one instruction exactly one right answer. BURGER turns language-conditioned drawing into a robot-learning benchmark: the canvas starts blank, so the policy cannot read the goal off the image, and every instruction admits many valid strokes. We generate demonstrations from text prompts at essentially unlimited scale, then use them to take five VLA models apart.

Why drawing?

VLA models are supposed to get better as robot data gets bigger and more diverse. Testing that claim needs datasets whose scale and diversity you can actually turn up — and physical demonstrations are too expensive to collect that way. Drawing gives us a data regime with four properties that manipulation benchmarks usually lack.

The canvas starts blank.
Nothing in the first observation hints at what to draw, so the policy has to get the task from the language. A common visual shortcut is closed off by construction.
Language complexity is a dial.
Prompts compose objects, attributes and spatial relations, so semantic difficulty can be increased systematically rather than by hand-authoring new scenes.
One instruction, many correct answers.
Stroke order, starting point and drawing direction all vary freely. This is genuine within-instruction multimodality — the thing a unimodal regressor provably cannot fit.
Failures are visible.
The finished canvas plus the execution trajectory make semantic, spatial and motor errors separately inspectable, instead of collapsing everything into one success bit.

We are not proposing drawing as a replacement for manipulation benchmarks. It abstracts away contact-rich interaction and object dynamics. It isolates a different set of capabilities — grounding, long-horizon continuous action, multimodality, data scaling — and measures those well.

From a text prompt to a robot trajectory

Every demonstration in BURGER is generated automatically. No human teleoperation is in the loop, which is what makes the dataset scalable.

  1. 1

    Prompt

    A bank of natural-language prompts — “draw a hamburger”, “draw a cloud on the left of a moon” — drawn from 132 single-object classes and 150 compositions.

  2. 2

    Prompt → PNG

    A text-to-image model renders a sketch-style raster image for the prompt, giving open-ended visual variety for the same words.

  3. 3

    Skeletonize

    Binarization, connected-component analysis and morphological skeletonization reduce the image to one-pixel-wide centerlines. Solid regions such as eyes become contours.

  4. 4

    Stroke order

    The skeleton is split into strokes and ordered by a heuristic that draws top-to-bottom, outlines before details, and prefers hopping to a nearby stroke next.

  5. 5

    Robot execution

    A simulated arm replays the strokes under an end-effector pose controller, with pen-lift transitions inserted between disconnected strokes. Marks appear when the tool approaches the canvas.

  6. 6

    Record

    RGB observations, proprioception and actions are logged as (observation, action) pairs in HDF5, then converted to standard robot-dataset formats for training.

One instruction, many trajectories

This is the claim that distinguishes BURGER from a manipulation benchmark, so we measured it rather than asserted it. Trajectories are resampled to 32 points, described by their start-relative xy path and step vectors, and clustered against a single shared k-means codebook fitted on the pooled data. Effective modes per instruction is exp(H) over that instruction's cluster histogram — 1.0 means every episode of the instruction takes the same route.

Isn't LIBERO just placing objects in fixed positions? (web-only analysis)

LIBERO's objects do move — but between instructions, not between episodes of one instruction. Measured from the grasp point over 38 instructions with a detectable grasp, within-instruction spread is 2.22 cm (median) while between-instruction spread is 18.95 cm, a factor of 8.6.

That is deliberate benchmark design, not missing randomization: LIBERO encodes spatial variation in the language, and the capability under test is grounding different phrases to different coordinates. The consequence matters for us, though. Cross-instruction variation asks a policy for a deterministic mapping, which a unimodal regressor satisfies completely. Within-instruction multimodality asks it to represent a distribution, which a unimodal regressor cannot — it converges to the conditional mean.

That is testable without training anything. Using the same cluster assignment, with the mode radius as the reference scale:

LIBERO's 0.00 is the key number: the conditional mean is a mode centre, because 78% of its instructions have only one mode. Averaging discards nothing, so a unimodal regressor and a distribution-modelling policy produce the same answer and the benchmark cannot separate them. On BURGER the mean sits 0.68 mode-radii out, in the gap between modes — the average of several valid drawings is not a valid drawing.

Honest counterweight: BURGER spans less 3-D volume than LIBERO (0.33×), because drawing is planar — z thickness is 0.23×. That is a depth effect, not an in-plane one. The pooled xy footprints nearly coincide (1.02×), and within a single instruction BURGER sweeps 2.5× more canvas area. Action-space cluster counts favour LIBERO, but are not comparable: BURGER holds 4 of 7 action dimensions constant by task design (three rotations and pen state), so that metric measures the action parameterization, not behavioural richness.

Scoring a drawing

Grading drawings is awkward precisely because they are multimodal: the same object can be rendered with different shapes and stroke orders and still be correct. Global image–text metrics miss fine-grained errors — CLIPScore will happily reward “an apple to the right of a pear” when the drawing shows the opposite.

So we build a sketch-specific metric space on a frozen DINOv3 backbone. The CLS token and mean-pooled patch embeddings are concatenated and mapped by a small two-layer MLP into a normalized sketch embedding, trained with a cosine classifier under classification and supervised-contrastive objectives so drawings of one class cluster tightly. Each class is then represented by the prototype of its training embeddings, and a generated drawing is scored by similarity to its target prototype. For compositional prompts we split the canvas at the minimum-connectivity boundary between the two foreground regions, score each object separately, and check that their spatial relation matches the instruction.

What BURGER shows about VLA models

We evaluate π0, π0.5, π0-FAST, OpenVLA and OpenVLA-OFT — spanning flow-matching, autoregressive and parallel-decoding action generation.

Scaling helps, but not uniformly

Sweeping training classes from 10 to 132 and demonstrations per class from 10 to 100, drawing quality generally improves along both axes — clearly for the flow-matching models π0 and π0.5. π0-FAST fluctuates more and does not consistently benefit, appearing more sensitive to the multimodal data distribution.

Grounding works in-distribution, and stops at its edge

Across trained categories, different instructions from the same blank canvas produce the right different objects, with no evident confusion between them. Name a category that was not in training and the models do not produce it — they fall back on similar unstructured strokes across different unseen prompts. Grounding is solid for learned concepts and does not extend beyond them.

Spatial relations transfer to unseen pairs

Trained on compositional tasks, policies produce valid left–right and over–under compositions for object pairs never seen together — a cup to the left of an apple, for instance — and distinguish the different relational instructions. The relation is learned as a relation, not memorized per pair.

Flow-matching policies keep their modes

Applying the same clustering to π0.5 rollouts: where the training data for four representative tasks holds ≈7.5 effective modes per instruction, the trained policy retains 3.6–5.1. It learns a distribution of valid drawings rather than one route.

Action chunking buys coherence, not coverage

In a controlled circle-drawing study — 100 demos sharing shape and radius while varying centre, start point and direction — OpenVLA keeps canvas contact but loses planar progress, while OpenVLA-OFT at chunk length 1 often fails to establish stable contact. Going from chunk 1 to 8 substantially improves contact and trajectory coherence. But coherent execution is not mode coverage: OFT with chunks and π0-FAST concentrate on a subset of start points and directions, while π0 and π0.5 preserve the diversity. Chunking governs execution coherence; the action-generation formulation governs multimodality.

Each model fails its own way

OpenVLA and π0-FAST are sensitive to the initial end-effector position — OpenVLA can get trapped in a local region, π0-FAST can drift off the target area. Flow-matching policies instead sometimes fail to stop after finishing the object, overpainting the canvas. On compositional prompts, object quality can degrade even when the spatial relation is right, and more so once the instruction space widens from horizontal to both horizontal and vertical relations — holding relational grounding and object geometry together is harder than either alone.

On a real Franka Panda

The real-robot dataset follows the same format as the simulated one, with two RealSense views from the front and back of the workspace; either can serve as the visual input. We collect 10 single-object classes at 10 demonstrations each and fine-tune π0.5 directly on it. The resulting policy executes real drawing tasks reliably, stays effective under camera-viewpoint changes, and closes the loop on canvas state: given a partially drawn cup it adds the missing handle, and when a human draws the rear wheel of a bicycle it skips that component and continues with the rest.

Rollouts are the front camera view of the deployment recordings, trimmed and re-encoded for the web. Each plays on hover or tap.

Limitations

The physics is simplified. Unlike robotic painting systems that model brush dynamics, BURGER targets sketch-level stroke generation with a rigid drawing tool. That is what makes it scalable, and it means the benchmark says nothing about contact-rich artistic media.

The data inherits the generator's biases. Sketches come from a text-to-image model and stroke order from our heuristic, so the dataset may encode drawing styles that differ from how people actually draw. It should not be read as a model of human drawing behaviour.

It is a diagnostic, not a substitute. Drawing abstracts away object dynamics and much of the uncertainty that makes manipulation hard. BURGER is meant to sit alongside manipulation benchmarks and isolate capabilities they measure poorly.
