Abstract

How effectively can vision-language-action (VLA) models learn diverse language-conditioned behaviours as training data scales? Answering this needs large, varied robot datasets, which are costly to collect on hardware. We introduce BURGER, a language-conditioned robotic drawing benchmark with a scalable, prompt-driven pipeline that generates continuous drawing trajectories in simulation and in the real world. An agent starts from the same blank canvas and draws the object or spatial composition named in the instruction, and every instruction admits an open-ended variety of valid drawings and stroke orders. Using 28,200 simulated demonstrations over 282 tasks, we evaluate five representative VLA models across data scale, language grounding and multimodal behaviour. Some VLAs benefit from more task diversity and more demonstrations, capture multimodal drawing behaviour, and generalise to unseen compositions of familiar objects, while the canvases expose distinct failure patterns per model. Finally, we deploy a trained policy on a Franka Panda that draws reactively under human intervention.

Motivation

Drawing is a controlled, scalable test of whether a VLA learns a distribution of behaviours or memorises one route.

VLA models increasingly benefit from larger and more diverse data, but testing whether an architecture can actually use such data needs datasets with controllable scale and diversity, and evaluations that separate different kinds of failure. Robotic drawing gives exactly that, for three reasons:

  1. No visual shortcut. Every episode starts from the same blank canvas, so the goal has to come from the language.
  2. Many right answers. One instruction admits many drawings that differ in shape, stroke order, start point and direction. A policy that averages them draws nothing recognisable.
  3. Failures you can see. The canvas is the record: semantic, spatial and execution errors stay visible on paper.

The second point is what sets BURGER apart from manipulation benchmarks, so we measure it directly.

How routes are counted

Planar end-effector paths, count-matched to 40 instructions × ≈42 episodes per dataset (all four LIBERO suites). Each path is resampled to 32 points and described by its start-relative xy path plus step vectors. One shared k-means codebook (k = 24) is fitted on the pooled trajectories of both datasets, and Neff = exp(−Σj pj log pj), where pj is the share of the instruction's trajectories in cluster j. The two distributions overlap at a single pair: LIBERO's most varied instruction (open the middle drawer of the cabinet, 1.96) against BURGER's least (draw a smiley face, 1.61).

Method

A prompt-driven pipeline turns text into robot trajectories, so data scale and diversity are controllable.

2.1 From a prompt to a robot trajectory

Demonstrations are generated, not teleoperated. A text-to-image model draws a line-art sketch; the sketch is skeletonised into one-pixel strokes, which are ordered (top to bottom, outline before details, nearest stroke next); an end-effector pose controller replays the strokes with pen lifts between them while every (observation, action) pair is recorded. Pick an object to follow one real sample through each stage.

2.2 Simulator-agnostic

2.3 The BURGER dataset

282 tasks × 100 demonstrations = 28,200 trajectories. 132 single objects, chosen from the Quick, Draw! categories as everyday things an arm can draw, and 150 compositions pairing base objects with a spatial relation (100 left of, 50 over / under). Stored as HDF5 and converted to standard robot-learning formats.

2.4 A drawing metric that agrees with people

CLIP-style scores measure global image–text alignment and miss fine errors, such as two objects on the wrong sides. We train a lightweight projection head on a frozen DINOv3 backbone so that drawings of a class cluster together, and score a drawing by its similarity to the target-class prototype. Compositions are split at the minimum-connectivity boundary, so each object and the relation are checked separately. In a user study, 66 participants ranked five drawings for each of 10 classes.

Experiments

Five VLAs, three questions: do they scale, do they compose, and do they keep the modes?

We fine-tune π0, π0.5 and π0-FAST (flow-matching and FAST-tokenised policies from the OpenPI family), OpenVLA (autoregressive) and OpenVLA-OFT (parallel-decoded action chunks) on BURGER, and score every final canvas with the metric of Section 2.4.

3.1 More classes and more demonstrations help, for some models

Training sets vary from 10 to 132 object classes and from 10 to 100 demonstrations per class. For π0 and π0.5, increasing either generally raises the drawing score. π0-FAST fluctuates more and does not consistently benefit from more data, which suggests it is more sensitive to the multimodal data distribution.

3.2 Spatial relations transfer to unseen object pairs

Policies trained on A left of B, A over B and A under B produce the requested layout for object pairs never seen together in training, and respond to the relation word. Object quality degrades under composition, more so with vertical relations, even when the layout is right.

3.3 Diagnosis: can your VLA even draw a circle?

To isolate action generation, we strip the task to one shape: 100 demonstrations share the circle's shape and radius but vary its centre, start point and direction (56 % clockwise). Each model runs 20 rollouts from randomised initial poses. Two findings follow. Chunking fixes coherence: OpenVLA keeps the pen down but loses planar progress, OFT with chunk length 1 rarely makes stable contact, and chunk length 8 restores coherent strokes. But not mode coverage: OFT and π0-FAST collapse onto a few start points, and π0-FAST draws clockwise in 19 of 20 runs. The flow-matching π0.5 keeps the balance.

3.4 On real object classes, failures differ by model

OpenVLA and π0-FAST are sensitive to the initial end-effector pose: OpenVLA can get trapped in a local region, π0-FAST can drift away from the target area. Flow-matching policies sometimes don't stop once the object is finished and overpaint it. These failures are visible on the canvas, which a success rate alone cannot show. After training, π0.5 keeps 3.6–5.1 effective modes per instruction in its own rollouts, against ≈7.5 in the data.

Limitations

  • Simplified physics. BURGER targets sketch-level strokes with a rigid drawing tool. It doesn't model brush dynamics or contact-rich media. That's what makes it scalable, and also what it can't test.
  • Inherited biases. Sketches come from a text-to-image model and stroke order from a heuristic, so the data may encode drawing styles that differ from how people draw. It isn't a model of human drawing behaviour.
  • Unseen objects. Policies follow trained object names without confusing them, but an object name absent from training yields similar unstructured strokes. Language grounding here covers composition of known concepts, not new ones.
