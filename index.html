BURGER Benchmarking and Understanding Robotic Generalization via Embodied dRawing

Anonymous authors · paper under double-blind review

Paper (PDF) arXiv · soon Code · soon Dataset · soon Video

A robot starts from a blank canvas and an instruction like draw a hamburger. There is no single right answer: every instruction admits many valid drawings, stroke orders and routes. That makes drawing a controlled, scalable way to ask whether a VLA policy has learned a distribution of behaviours or memorised one.

The idea

Why draw?

1

No visual shortcut

Every episode starts from the same blank canvas. The goal can't be read off the first image — it has to come from the language.

2

Many right answers

One instruction admits many valid drawings that differ in shape, stroke order, start point and direction. A policy that averages them draws nothing recognisable.

3

Failures you can see

The canvas is the record. Semantic, spatial and execution failures stay visible on paper, so you can diagnose them, not just count them.

BURGER isn't a replacement for manipulation benchmarks. Drawing abstracts away contact-rich interaction and object dynamics. What it isolates is language grounding, long-horizon continuous action and multimodal behaviour, at a data scale that would be costly to collect on hardware.

Key evidence

One instruction, many valid routes

We overlay every demonstration of a single instruction as planar end-effector paths, all aligned at their start point, then count how many distinct routes they take. Most LIBERO instructions take one route. BURGER instructions take about seven on average.

Not cherry-picked

Every instruction of the count-matched comparison is plotted above (40 per dataset; 42 episodes each for BURGER, 29–42 for LIBERO). 31 of LIBERO's 40 score exactly 1.00. The two distributions overlap at a single pair: LIBERO's most varied instruction (open the middle drawer of the cabinet, 1.96) against BURGER's least (draw a smiley face, 1.61).

Not an artefact of k

LIBERO stays at 1.05–1.10 at every clustering resolution. BURGER keeps rising, which is what real multimodality looks like. The ratio depends on k, so we report it at the paper's k = 24: 7.13 vs 1.09, 6.6×.

Why it matters

On LIBERO the average of an instruction's demonstrations is a valid trajectory, so a policy that regresses to the mean and one that models the full distribution look the same. On BURGER the average lands between routes. Toggle Show the average path to see it: the mean of several valid drawings is not a drawing.

How the routes are counted

Planar end-effector paths, count-matched to 40 instructions × ≈42 episodes per dataset, using all four LIBERO suites. Each path is resampled to 32 points and described by its start-relative xy path plus step vectors. One shared k-means codebook (k = 24) is fitted on the pooled trajectories of both datasets, and the effective number of modes per instruction is Neff = exp(−Σj pj log pj), where pj is the share of that instruction's trajectories in cluster j. 1.0 means every episode takes the same route. The explorer above shows the actual trajectories and cluster assignments behind the paper's Fig. 2.

Method

From a prompt to a robot trajectory

Each demonstration is generated, not teleoperated. A text prompt becomes a sketch, the sketch becomes ordered strokes, and a robot arm replays the strokes on a canvas while every observation and action is recorded. Pick an object to follow one sample through each stage.

  1. draw a bicycle

    1Prompt

    An object or spatial composition, phrased as an instruction.

  2. 2Sketch

    A text-to-image model renders a line-art PNG from the prompt.

  3. 3Skeleton

    Binarise, extract connected components and skeletonise to one-pixel strokes. Filled regions such as eyes become outlines.

  4. 4Ordered strokes

    Split into strokes and order them: top to bottom, outline before details, nearby strokes next. Darker strokes come later.

  5. 5Robot execution

    An end-effector pose controller follows the strokes in simulation, with pen lifts between them. (observation, action) pairs are recorded at every step.

Sketch, skeleton and strokes above are the real intermediate files for sample 001 of each class. The video is that same SVG replayed in ManiSkill / SAPIEN. The skeleton is re-run with the pipeline's own script, and its lines are thickened for display.

The same task in three simulators

The trajectory-generation interface isn't tied to one engine. One SVG task, executed in SAPIEN, MuJoCo and Isaac Sim through each simulator's own robot-control interface. 3× speed

Results

What five VLA models learn from drawing

We evaluate π0, π0.5 and π0-FAST (flow-matching and FAST-tokenised policies from the OpenPI family), OpenVLA (autoregressive) and OpenVLA-OFT (parallel-decoded action chunks). Drawings are scored with a DINOv3-based metric checked against human rankings (see Evaluation metric).

More classes, more demonstrations: better drawings, for some models

Training sets vary from 10 to 132 object classes and from 10 to 100 demonstrations per class. For π0 and π0.5, increasing either generally raises the drawing score. π0-FAST fluctuates more and doesn't consistently benefit from more data, which suggests it is more sensitive to the multimodal data distribution.

Y-axis: mean similarity to the target-class prototype under our metric. Figure reproduced from the paper (Fig. 6).

Spatial relations transfer to object pairs never seen together

Policies trained on compositional prompts (draw A left of B, A over B, A under B) produce valid left–right and over–under layouts for pairs that never appear in training, and respond correctly to the relation word. Object quality degrades under composition, more so once vertical relations are added, even when the layout is right.

A metric that agrees with people

CLIP-style scores measure global image–text alignment and can miss fine errors, like a drawing with the two objects on the wrong sides. We train a lightweight projection head on a frozen DINOv3 backbone so that drawings of the same class cluster together, then score a drawing by its similarity to the target-class prototype. Compositions are split at the minimum-connectivity boundary, so each object and the relation between them are checked separately.

In a user study, 66 participants ranked five drawings for each of 10 classes: four from VLA policies and one reference.

Diagnosis

Can your VLA even draw a circle?

Strip the task down to one shape. 100 demonstrations share the same circle shape and radius but vary its centre, start point and direction. Five policies, 20 rollouts each, from randomised initial poses. The canvas shows each model's failure plainly.

Chunking fixes coherence…

OpenVLA keeps the pen on the paper but loses planar progress. OpenVLA-OFT with chunk length 1 often never makes stable contact. Raising the chunk length from 1 to 8 restores contact and coherent strokes.

…but not mode coverage

Coherent isn't the same as diverse. OFT with chunks and π0-FAST concentrate on a subset of start points and directions. π0-FAST draws clockwise in 19 of 20 runs, against a 56 / 44 split in the data. The flow-matching π0.5 keeps the balance.

On real objects, failures differ by model

OpenVLA and π0-FAST are sensitive to the initial end-effector pose: OpenVLA can get trapped in a local region, and π0-FAST can drift away from the target area. Flow-matching policies sometimes don't stop once the object is finished and overpaint it. These failures read the same on every canvas, which is what a success rate alone can't show. Paper, Fig. 8.

Scope

Limitations

  • Simplified physics. BURGER targets sketch-level strokes with a rigid drawing tool. It doesn't model brush dynamics or contact-rich media. That's what makes it scalable, and also what it can't test.
  • Inherited biases. Sketches come from a text-to-image model and stroke order from a heuristic, so the data may encode drawing styles that differ from how people draw. It isn't a model of human drawing behaviour.
  • Unseen objects. Policies follow trained object names without confusing them, but an object name absent from training yields similar unstructured strokes. Language grounding here covers composition of known concepts, not new ones.
